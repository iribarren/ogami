# Cards and Dice as Oracles

A fortune teller who lays out a row of cards and reads your future in them is putting a question to a deck, and you can put your own questions to the world in the same way without a printed table. A deck of playing cards and a handful of dice are enough, and each does something tables do badly. Cards, for one, run out as you draw them, and a throw of several dice can be read in more than one way.

Here we use those two tools as oracles in their own right. When you want a specific line, a table is still the right tool. Cards and dice suit the moments when you want something looser, or when you do not have the table with you.

## A deck of cards

A standard deck of 52 gives you four suits and thirteen ranks. Decide once what they mean, and write it down on an index card, or a page of your notebook.

**Draw to answer.** Shuffle and draw one card. Red is yes and black is no, and the rank gives the size of the answer, with a low card a small one and a face card a big one. An ace is always the extreme, whichever way it falls. That gives you a likelihood oracle with a "how much" already built into it.

**Draw to ask what.** Give each suit a kind of thing, say hearts for people, spades for danger, clubs for places and diamonds for things you can carry. A draw then tells you which kind of thing is about to turn up, and the rank tells you how close it is, from a two that is still far off to a king that is already in the room.

**Draw to count.** When you need a number, such as the guards at the gate, the days a journey will take or the weight of a purse, use the rank, with an ace as one and a face card as ten.

### Drawing and reshuffling

Unlike a die, a deck remembers what it has already told you, and that memory is what makes it useful. The memory comes from drawing without putting back, so that each card you turn is gone. After twenty draws the deck has told you what it will no longer say, and a suit that has run out is an answer in itself. Keep the used cards face up in a discard pile beside the deck, where you can see what has been spent.

Reshuffle when the story turns rather than out of habit, as it does with a new session, a new Phase or a change of place, and the deck will then tell you about the beginning again.

You can also seed a card you want. Put it, say the king of spades, in the bottom third and shuffle the rest on top, and it will come, although not yet. This is the trick behind the paper deck of Part I. The road deck of The Long Carry seeds the Shrine Gate card near the bottom, so the end comes late, but not at the same place twice.

## Handfuls of dice

A few dice thrown at once give you more than one number, and the answer lies in how the numbers spread.

**Odds and evens.** Throw three dice and count the evens. Three evens is a clean yes and none is a clean no, while one or two is a "yes, but" or a "no, but", as you like.

**Highest and lowest.** Throw several dice and take the highest and the lowest. The highest is how good it gets and the lowest is how bad, and the gap between them is how much is at stake.

**Pairs.** When two dice match, something has an echo. Decide before you play what an echo means, whether a repeat, a witness or a coincidence.

**A pool for a count.** Throw a handful and count the dice that show 5 or 6, which gives you the number of things going wrong, or going right.

Whichever you use, choose the rule before you throw and write it down, because a rule you make up after the dice have landed will be one that agrees with what you wanted.

## Choosing between them

Reach for the tool that fits the question.

| You want                       | Use                                |
| ------------------------------ | ---------------------------------- |
| A yes or no with a size        | A single card, red or black        |
| A specific line of prose       | A table and one die                |
| Something that gets used up    | A deck, drawn without putting back |
| A spread: how good and how bad | A handful of dice                  |

Mixing them is fine when one leads to the other, as when you draw a card for the kind of thing and then roll a die on the table for that kind. Do not mix two systems within one question, though, or you will not remember which result you were reading.

## On paper

Keep an index card, or a page of your notebook, headed **Cards and dice**, with the rule for each tool on one line, saying what red and black mean, what each suit is and what odds and evens do. If you use a card, tape it inside the lid of the box.

When you draw, write the card in the margin beside the line it answered. A king of clubs in the margin then reminds you that the clubs have been used.

A table does the work of a deck. A weighted table is a deck in which every entry has as many copies as its **Weight**. Every roll on it is fresh: nothing is removed, and there is no discard pile or reshuffle. Draw a physical deck at the table if you want a pile that runs out.

## The Long Carry

On paper, The Long Carry is the clearest use of a deck. Its thirteen road cards stand for the five Scene Types of the road, with three Camps, two Fords, three Strangers, two Ruins and three Omens, and the Shrine Gate card is shuffled into the bottom five.

Four cards have gone so far, a Camp, an Omen, a Stranger and another Camp, and the player is about to turn the fifth.

> I turn the card: a Ford. I have drawn two Camps and no Fords. The deck had two Fords, so the river is coming whether I am ready or not. I put the Ford face up on the discard pile and begin the scene.

The player could see the river coming because the deck remembers where they have been, which a die does not.

## Cold Berth

::image{file="dw-cards-deck.webp" style="dogwatch" size="spot" alt="Five face-down cards fanned on a metal deck, one set aside." subject="Five face-down cards fanned on a bare metal deck plate, a sixth card set apart from them, all backs blank."}

Cold Berth has no deck for its scenes, since its four job cards lie face up and the player picks one, which makes the choice itself the oracle. **The Ship** needs no deck, but its compartments make a small one, with a card for each compartment you named, carrying its name, and when you would rather not pick a place you can shuffle those cards and draw.

There are five compartment cards, for the Galley, the Hold, the Bridge, Cryo and the Corridor. The player picks Investigate, which asks for a place, and wants the place chosen for them.

> I shuffle the five cards and draw one: Cryo. I turn it up, and set it aside. The next job cannot go there without a reason.

The draw chose where this job happens, and since the card is set aside, the small deck now also says where the next one cannot go without a reason.

## Try it

Set aside a deck and write one line for what red and black mean, one for what each suit means and one for when you reshuffle. Then play ten draws and keep the ones that made a scene.
