# Cards and Print

On paper a flow is a pile of cards. This chapter is about making the pile: what goes on a card, how you draw from it, how to make the end of the story turn up late, and what to do with a card once it is played.

## Card anatomy

There are two kinds of card.

**A Scene Type card** is the one from the Scene Types chapter.

- **Front:** the name, the purpose and the tips.
- **Back:** Setup, Play and Closing, each with its questions or its roll.

Print the name large, because you will be finding cards by name. Keep the back to what fits. A step that takes a paragraph does not belong on a card.

**A table card** is for a deck that stands in for an oracle table. It has one line on the front, the text of the entry, and the name of the Scene Type it starts. A table of eight entries is eight cards. Where an entry carries a weight, the card is printed once for each copy.

Cards are cheap, so give every Scene Type its own and keep the rest on a single sheet: the clocks, the slots, the closing check and the endings. That sheet is the flow's rules card. Chapter 7 describes what goes on it.

## Drawing

You can draw in two ways, and they suit different jobs.

- **In order.** Take the top card, every time. This is a sequence. The deck is already shuffled, by the author, and the order is part of the design.
- **At random.** Shuffle, and draw. This is an oracle deck. The order is the dice.

A third way sits between them. Shuffle the deck, then lay out a few cards face up and choose one. This is a spread with a random menu: you decide, but only among what the dice offered.

## Discard or reshuffle

After a card is played, it goes one of two places.

**Back into the deck, reshuffled.** Every draw is a fresh draw, and the odds do not change. This is what the Choosing the Next Scene chapter assumed. Nothing is remembered, so three Fords in a row are possible.

**Onto a discard pile.** The deck gets shorter, and the odds shift with it. When it runs out you either stop or reshuffle the discard. A deck that you discard from is also a clock: the shorter it gets, the nearer the end.

Reshuffling suits a world that does not remember, like a road. Discarding suits a story that should run out.

## Seeding a finale

If the deck is a clock, you can place the ending in it. Before the first draw, take the finale card and shuffle it into only the bottom part of the deck: the last third, or the last five cards. The ending can then turn up late, and it cannot turn up early. It does not turn up at a time you chose, which is why it feels found.

Count the cards and place the finale to match the length you want. A deck of fifteen with the finale in the bottom five ends between the tenth and the fifteenth draw.

## On paper

The Long Carry's deck is the road deck: thirteen copies of five cards, with the Shrine Gate card shuffled into the bottom of the pile. Discard as you play. The road ends when the gate turns up or when the closing check on Winter ends it, whichever comes first.

Cold Berth needs no deck for its finale. The Reveal comes from Threat, a clock you can see, so a Ship round never needs to hide an ending in a pile. Its cards are the four job cards, face up, and a card for each compartment you named in Session Zero.

Make the cards yourself: an index card for each Scene Type, and a deck with the right number of copies.

:::in-ogami
Ogami has no deck. A draw is a roll on the oracle table that the Phase names, so the road deck is The road deck table, and each copy is a point of its **Weight**. Every roll is a fresh one: there is no discard pile and nothing is removed from the table.

The paper deck's seeded finale has no counterpart. The Shrine Gate is not a card. The Road ends through two steps in its **Scene closing**: a **condition** on Winter, then a **roll** of a d6, and each has a band with an `endPhase` effect. The Shrine Gate is the next Phase, played in sequence.

In Play, the scene pick shows the Scene Type's purpose and tips on the card it offers.
:::

## The Long Carry

On paper The Long Carry plays out of a pile, and the pile is a clock. Thirteen road cards, and the Shrine Gate somewhere in the bottom five. Every card played is a step nearer the gate, and you cannot say which step it will be.

A played draw, from the paper deck. Nine cards are on the discard. Five remain, and the Shrine Gate is among them.

> I turn the top card: _Omen: the road says something about the saint._ I play it.
>
> Next card: _Ruin: what the road left behind._ Then the Camp. Then the Shrine Gate.

The gate arrived late, and no one chose the day. The pile did.

## Cold Berth

The Ship needs no deck, but the compartments make a small one. Each compartment is a card with its name and a line about it. In a round you lay them out face up and take one, or you shuffle them and draw. Laying them out is a choice. Shuffling them is how a ship feels when it is out of your hands.

A played draw. The crew's three compartment cards are the Wake Bay, the Drive Spine and the Cold Hold. I shuffle them, because I do not trust my own choice.

> I draw the Cold Hold. I had wanted the Drive Spine, which is the loud one. The Cold Hold is where the fourth sleeper is, and I have been avoiding it.

Letting the pile choose is a way to make yourself go where you were not going to.

## Try it

Count the Scene Types in your flow. Decide for each Phase whether its cards are drawn in order, at random or from a spread, and whether a played card is reshuffled or discarded. If you want the ending to turn up late, decide how many cards from the bottom you will seed it.
