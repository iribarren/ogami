# Inspiration and Random Events

An oracle that says yes or no keeps a story going only while your questions have yes or no answers. Sooner or later you stare at a blank line and the question starts with "what". Who is this person, what is in the room, what has just gone wrong?

You reach for **inspiration** when that question has you stuck. A **random event** comes from the other side, since it interrupts you when you are not stuck at all.

## Inspiration

A writing class sometimes hands out a few random words and asks for a story built on them, and inspiration works the same way. You roll for a prompt, a few words, a line or a pair of ideas, and you do the rest yourself.

**Word pairs.** Make two short lists of words, say twelve each, one of actions and one of things, and roll one die on each. "Hide" and "bell" is a start, and "lend" and "road" is a better one. Make the lists from your own game, since a bearer's words are not a crew member's. The pair is only a seed, so write the first thing it makes you think of and stop there.

**Prompts.** A table of questions works the same way. A question such as "What does the stranger want that they will not say?" gives you less than a list of names would, although it gives you more than an empty page.

**Lines.** The tables in a flow are made of lines of this kind, such as _A boy with a bell and no one to ring it for_. A line like that is specific enough to see and open enough to argue with.

## Reading a roll

A roll gives you the beginning of a sentence, and these steps finish it.

1. **Say the first meaning.** Whatever comes to you first is usually the most honest.
2. **Tie it to what you know.** Ask which Thread it touches and which person in the notebook would care, because a result that connects to something you have written is worth three that do not.
3. **Make it cost.** If the meaning is comfortable, ask what it takes from someone.
4. **Write it down in one line**, and then move on.

Do not roll again just because the first result was awkward. The awkward result is the one that surprises you. If it truly does not fit where you are, or gives you too little to work with, roll again, on the same table or on another one, and write down the first result as well as the new one. Keep an eye on how often you do it, though, because after too many rolls the first one can stop meaning anything.

### When you are stuck

Work through these in order and stop at the first one that helps.

1. Read your last journal line aloud and ask what the person in it wants.
2. Open a list and pick the entry that has waited longest.
3. Roll a prompt table, read the result and write one sentence.
4. Ask a yes or no question about the thing you are afraid to write.

## Random events

A **random event** is an interruption the world decides to make, and it is how you get the kind of surprise a game master gives, such as the knock at the door that nobody planned.

In a good event table the quiet result is the most common single result. Both flows give it three chances in nine, and that matters because a table that always interrupts teaches you to ignore it. The rest of the table makes something worse, which means a number moves, or it sends you into a scene you did not choose, and in Road Risks the risen ford does both at once.

### How often to call one

There are three honest ways to decide when an event comes. The steadiest is to roll the event table at a fixed point every turn, as the World turn does, and because that is predictable you can trust it. You can also roll on a trigger, whenever something happens, such as a failed check, an oracle answer on the edge or a scene that has run quiet for too long. The loosest way is to roll when the game feels flat, at the moment you notice you are narrating and not playing, although this is also the rule you will most easily forget.

Choose one and keep to it, because a game that rolls an event only when the player is comfortable is a game with no events.

## On paper

Keep the events table and the word lists on one page, with the die at the top. Roll at the point your flow names, and when the result is not a scene, write it in the margin as one line and mark any number it moves.

If you use word pairs, write down the pair, the sentence it gave you and the Thread it belongs to, because ten minutes later you will have forgotten why the words mattered.

## The Long Carry

The Long Carry has no word pairs, but its Omens table is inspiration, with eight lines that are each an image the saint has left behind. You roll it in the Omen scene, which then asks _What do you now believe the saint did?_

Here the Omens table, a d8, gives a 6.

> _Dogs that will not cross the shadow of the litter._ First meaning: they are afraid. Of what? I tie it to the Sin so far: a debt to a river. The dogs are not afraid of the saint. They are keeping out of his way, as you do with a man who owes.
>
> I write one line: "The saint is owed. The dogs know who by."

The table gave the player an image, and one line was enough to turn it into a belief.

## Cold Berth

Ship trouble is Cold Berth's event table, rolled in the World turn. Of its nine chances three are quiet, two raise Threat and four send you to a scene.

Threat has just gone up to four when the World turn rolls Ship trouble, and the d10 comes up 8.

> _A crew voice calls from a room that is locked from the outside._ The line sends me to an Investigate scene. First meaning: it is Ines, who is missing. Is it? I do not decide yet.
>
> I tie it to the Thread "Where is Ines?" and write: "A voice, not hers, using her cadence." The door is locked from outside. Someone has been talking to the ship.

A quiet result would have given the player nothing, whereas this one made a scene.

## Try it

Make a word list of twelve actions and twelve things from your own flow. Roll three pairs, write one sentence for each and keep the one that frightens you.
