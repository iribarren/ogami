# Inspiration and Random Events

An oracle that says yes or no is not enough to keep a story going. Sooner or later you stare at a blank line and the question is not yes or no. It is what. Who is this person? What is in the room? What just went wrong?

This chapter is about two tools for that: **inspiration**, which you reach for when you are stuck, and **random events**, which interrupt you when you are not.

## Inspiration

Inspiration is a prompt you roll for, not an answer you accept. You get a few words, a line or a pair of ideas, and you do the rest.

**Word pairs.** Make two short lists of words, say twelve each, one of actions and one of things. Roll one die on each. "Hide" and "bell" is a start. "Lend" and "road" is a better one. Make the lists from your own game: a bearer's words are not a crew member's. The pair is a seed, so write the first thing it makes you think of and stop.

**Prompts.** A table of questions works the same way. "What does the stranger want that they will not say?" gives you less than a list of names, and more than an empty page.

**Lines.** The tables in a flow are lines of this kind: _A boy with a bell and no one to ring it for_. They are specific enough to see and open enough to argue with.

## Reading a roll

The roll is the beginning of a sentence. To finish it:

1. **Say the first meaning.** Whatever comes to you first is usually the most honest.
2. **Tie it to what you know.** Which Thread does it touch? Which person in the notebook would care? A result that connects to something you have written is worth three that do not.
3. **Make it cost.** If the meaning is comfortable, ask what it takes from someone.
4. **Write it down in one line.** Then move on.

Do not roll twice because the first result was awkward. The awkward result is the one that surprises you. If it truly does not fit, roll again once, and write both.

### When you are stuck

Work through these in order and stop at the first that helps.

1. Read your last journal line aloud and ask what the person in it wants.
2. Open a list and pick the entry that has waited longest.
3. Roll a prompt table, read the result and write one sentence.
4. Ask a yes or no question about the thing you are afraid to write.

## Random events

A **random event** is an interruption the world decides to make. It is how you get the surprise a game master gives: the knock at the door nobody planned.

Two things make a good event table.

- **Most of it is quiet.** In both flows, three entries in nine say nothing happens. A table that always interrupts teaches you to ignore it.
- **It does one of two jobs.** It makes something worse, a number that moves, or it sends you into a scene you did not choose.

### How often to call one

There are three honest ways to decide.

- **Every turn.** Roll the event table at a fixed point, as the world turn does. It is predictable, so you can trust it.
- **On a trigger.** Roll when something happens: a failed check, an oracle answer on the edge, a scene that ran quiet for too long.
- **When it feels flat.** Roll when you notice you are narrating and not playing. This is the loosest rule and the easiest to forget.

Choose one and keep to it. A game that rolls an event only when the player is comfortable is a game with no events.

## On paper

Keep the events table and the word lists on one page, with the die at the top. Roll at the point your flow names. When the result is not a scene, write it in the margin as one line, and mark any number it moves.

If you use word pairs, write the pair, the sentence it gave you and the Thread it belongs to. Ten minutes later you will have forgotten why the words mattered.

## The Long Carry

The Long Carry has no word pairs, but its Omens table is inspiration of the first kind: eight lines, each an image the saint has left behind. The Omen scene rolls it and then asks _What do you now believe the saint did?_

A played roll. The Omens table, a d8, gives a 6.

> _Dogs that will not cross the shadow of the litter._ First meaning: they are afraid. Of what? I tie it to the Sin so far: a debt to a river. The dogs are not afraid of the saint. They are keeping out of his way, as you do with a man who owes.
>
> I write one line: "The saint is owed. The dogs know who by."

An image became a belief in a line.

## Cold Berth

Ship trouble is Cold Berth's event table, rolled in the world turn. Of its nine chances, three are quiet. Two raise Threat, and four send a scene.

A played event. Threat has just gone up to four, and the world turn rolls a d10: 8.

> _A crew voice calls from a room that is locked from the outside._ The line sends me to an Investigate scene. First meaning: it is Ines, who is missing. Is it? I do not decide yet.
>
> I tie it to the Thread "Where is Ines?" and write: "A voice, not hers, using her cadence." The door is locked from outside. Someone has been talking to the ship.

A quiet result would have given nothing. This one made a scene.

## Try it

Make a word list of twelve actions and twelve things from your own flow. Roll three pairs and write one sentence for each. Keep the one that frightens you.
