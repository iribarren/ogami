# Journaling and Lists

Solo play has one great weakness. When you play with other people, they remember for you. Alone, whatever you do not write down is gone by next week.

This chapter is about the two things that fix that: a **journal**, which keeps the story, and **lists**, which keep the cast. Together they are your memory and your table of contents.

## The journal

The journal is a log, in order, of what happened. Its job is not to be good writing. Its job is to let you pick up the game after a month and know where you are.

For each scene write:

- **A heading.** The session, the scene and the Scene Type. "Session 2, scene 5, Ford."
- **What you rolled.** The question, the odds, the answer; the table and the line.
- **What happened.** Two to five sentences in plain past tense.
- **What changed.** A Tracker that moved, a Thread opened, a name added to a list.

Write while you play, not afterwards. A scene written the next morning has lost its small surprises.

### What to write

Write what you will need and what you will enjoy.

- **Decisions.** What the character chose, and what they gave up to choose it.
- **Answers you did not expect.** A "no, but" you would not have written yourself is worth a full line.
- **Names, the first time.** The name, one detail and where it came from.
- **Promises.** Anything a person said they would do. These become Threads.

### What to let go

You cannot write everything, and you should not.

- **The ordinary.** If the bearers made camp and nothing happened, "Camp. Quiet." is the entry.
- **Rolls that changed nothing.** If a check did not move a Tracker or a scene, you do not need the dice.
- **Rejected ideas.** If you wrote a line and crossed it out, leave it crossed out.
- **Anything you can look up.** The table has the line. You need only its number.

When you are not sure, ask whether you would miss it in three sessions. If not, let it go.

## Lists

A **list** is a page that keeps one kind of thing. A solo game wants four.

| List    | Keeps                       | You add to it when                                  |
| ------- | --------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------- |
| Threads | Open questions              | A line asks one, a person makes a promise           |
| NPCs    | People, with what they want | A table gives someone, a scene needs a name         |
| Places  | Locations, with a detail    | A scene happens somewhere you may go back to        |
| Facts   | What is now true            | You decide something the story will have to respect |

Part I's chapter on Memory says what each is for. Here is how to keep them.

- **One line each.** A name and a clause. If you need a paragraph, you have two entries.
- **Close, never erase.** Strike through a Thread when it ends, an NPC when they leave. A struck name is still a name you can remember.
- **Prune on purpose.** At the end of each session read each list once. Close what cannot matter any more.
- **Roll on a list.** When you need a person who is already in the story, number the open NPCs and roll. It is the cheapest way to make the world feel small.

Keep Threads honest by weighting them. Mark the ones that matter most, and when you want a Thread to come back, roll on those first.

## On paper

Use one notebook with the journal at the front, and the four lists in the back, each on its own page. Leave room in the lists to cross things out. Date every entry.

At the end of a session, write three lines:

1. Where we are.
2. What is open.
3. What I want to see next.

You will thank yourself on the day you pick the notebook up.

:::in-ogami
In Play, the **Journal** records every roll and answer as it happens: dice rolls, oracle answers and table results. It is grouped by session and then by scene. To write something of your own, use **New note** and **Write note**. **Attach results** on the note composer lets you attach up to 20 earlier results to it.

The lists are Play panels: **Facts**, **Threads**, **Places** and **NPCs**. Each has an add button (**Add Thread**, **Add Place**, **Add NPC**, **Add fact**), and its entries can be edited, given a **Weight**, and closed or reopened with **Close** and **Reopen**. An NPC also has an **Agenda**.

A flow's steps can open Threads and NPCs for you, with effects such as `createThread` and `createNpc`, so they need not be typed twice.
:::

## The Long Carry

The Long Carry produces a lot of small material: a Stranger, a Ruin, an Omen. A journal for it is mostly the road, one line each, with the Sin rewritten every time an Omen changes it.

A journal entry for one scene might read:

> Session 1, scene 4. Omen. Omens: _Dogs that will not cross the shadow of the litter._ Sin: now "The saint is owed. The dogs know who by." Winter 5, Burden 1.

Keep the Threads page short. Put the question of the whole game on its first line, "Will we reach the shrine before snow?", and let it stand until the Epilogue. Add another only when a Stranger makes a promise.

## Cold Berth

Cold Berth opens Threads quickly, because its trouble is people. The ship takes a crew member each round, and a Thread, "Where is Ines?", opens for each.

A journal entry for one round might read:

> Session 2, scene 6. Investigate, the Galley. Treason: a falsified cryo log. Suspect: Pell. Clues 3. Thread: Who falsified the cryo log? Ines lost. Threat 5.

The notebook has two lists that matter at the table: the manifest of NPCs tagged crew, which the ship takes from, and the list of Places tagged compartment, which every job asks for. The crew list shrinks as the loop goes on. That is the point.

## Try it

Write a journal entry for the last scene you played, in five lines. Then cross out every line you would not miss in three sessions. What is left is what you should have written.
