# Likelihood Oracles

A game master answers a thousand small questions a night. Is the door locked? Does the guard know your face? Is anyone home? Most of the time the answer comes from the story that is already on the table. Sometimes it does not, and you are the only one in the room.

A **likelihood oracle** is a way to ask yourself a yes or no question and let a die answer. You say how likely you think the answer is, you roll once, and you read what comes up. It is a tool for the moments when you honestly do not know.

## The procedure

This book uses one die, a d6, and five named odds.

| Odds         | Yes on | Plain yes | Plain no |
| ------------ | ------ | --------- | -------- |
| Long shot    | 1      | none      | 3 to 6   |
| Unlikely     | 1 or 2 | 1         | 4 to 6   |
| Even         | 1 to 3 | 1 or 2    | 5 or 6   |
| Likely       | 1 to 4 | 1 to 3    | 6        |
| Near certain | 1 to 5 | 1 to 4    | none     |

Picture a line between the last yes face and the first no face. The two faces that touch the line carry a **but**.

- The last face of the yes is **yes, but**. The thing is so, and it costs you something, or it is not quite what you pictured.
- The first face of the no is **no, but**. The thing is not so, and the story hands you something back: a way round, a clue, an ally.

A Long shot that comes in is always a yes, but, and a Near certain that fails is always a no, but. That is on purpose. A surprise should leave a mark.

## Setting the odds

Odds are your honest guess about the fiction, not about what you want. Ask three things.

1. **What is the world like?** A locked door in a quiet house is Likely to be locked. A locked door in a house on fire is not.
2. **What do the people in it want?** A guard who was paid to look away is Likely to look away.
3. **What have I already written?** If an earlier line says the road is empty, a crowd on it is a Long shot.

If you cannot decide, say Even and roll. Do not spend longer on the odds than on the question.

## Reading a "but"

The but is yours to write, and you write it from the question. Ask what would make the answer true, and what that would cost.

- A yes, but pays a price: it takes time, it is seen, it breaks something, it leaves a debt.
- A no, but is a door closing with a window beside it: you cannot, yet you notice, or someone offers.

Keep it to one sentence. If the but needs a paragraph, you have started a scene, and the flow should run it.

## When to ask and when to decide

Ask when both answers would make a good story and you do not know which you want to read. Decide when:

- the story has already answered it. Do not roll to see whether a door exists if you have written the door.
- one answer is dull and the other is not. Take the interesting one, and keep moving.
- it is a question about your own bearer or crew member. What your character does is not a roll. What the world does to them is.

Rewrite open questions as closed ones. "Who is at the door?" is a table or a decision. "Is it someone I know?" is a yes or no. Ask the closed one and let the answer prompt the open one.

Roll rarely. Three or four oracle questions in a scene is plenty. More than that and you are reading the dice, not the story.

## On paper

Keep a half page headed **Questions**. For each one write the question, the odds, the die and the answer, in one line.

> Is the keeper expecting us? Unlikely, 3: no, but.

Write the but beside it. A page of these is a record of the surprises you did not choose.

Do not erase a question you did not like the answer to. Cross the answer out only if you made an error in the roll, and say so.

## The Long Carry

The Long Carry does not ask a yes or no question of its own. The player supplies them, on the road. The best are about what the bearers meet.

A played question. The Road has turned up a Ruin, a farm with the table laid for twelve. The player wonders whether anyone is still in it.

> Is anyone still in the farm? Unlikely, the road has been quiet, and the place has been empty a long time. I roll a d6: 2. A plain yes would have been a 1, so this is the last yes face: yes, but.
>
> Yes, but. There is one old woman, and she does not say who she laid the table for. She has already set a place for the saint.

The unlikely answer came in, so the but is heavy. The scene now has a person and a mystery.

## Cold Berth

In Cold Berth the questions come from the Reveal and the Truths. The crew writes a hunch, and a likelihood oracle tests how well it fits the ship.

A played question. In Investigate, the player suspects the custodian is covering for someone.

> Is the custodian lying? Likely, it has refused every question about the empty berth. I roll a d6: 5. That is the first face of the no: no, but.
>
> No, but. It is telling the truth about the berth. What it does not say is how it knows.

A no that holds a clue. Write the line in the notebook and keep the question open.

## Try it

Write three yes or no questions your own flow is going to raise, and set odds for each. Then ask for each one answer you would be happy with and one you would not. If you are happy with both, you did not need to roll.
