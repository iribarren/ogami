# Likelihood Oracles

A game master answers a thousand small questions a night. Is the door locked? Does the guard know your face? Is anyone home? Most of the time the story so far already holds the answer, but sometimes it does not, and when you play alone you are the only one in the room.

If you have ever tossed a coin because you could not settle something yourself, you know the idea already. A **likelihood oracle** lets you ask yourself a yes or no question and leave the answer to a die. It works like the coin, except that before you roll you say how likely you think the answer is, and then you roll once and read what comes up. It is a tool for the moments when you honestly do not know.

## The procedure

This book uses one die, a d6, and five named odds.

| Odds         | Yes on | Plain yes | Plain no |
| ------------ | ------ | --------- | -------- |
| Long shot    | 1      | none      | 3 to 6   |
| Unlikely     | 1 or 2 | 1         | 4 to 6   |
| Even         | 1 to 3 | 1 or 2    | 5 or 6   |
| Likely       | 1 to 4 | 1 to 3    | 6        |
| Near certain | 1 to 5 | 1 to 4    | none     |

Picture a line between the last yes face and the first no face. The two faces that touch the line carry a **but**. The last face of the yes is **yes, but**, which means that the thing is so, although it costs you something or is not quite what you pictured. The first face of the no is **no, but**, which means that the thing is not so, and the story hands you something back instead, such as a way round, a clue or an ally.

A Long shot that comes in is always a yes, but, and a Near certain that fails is always a no, but. We placed the faces that way on purpose, because a surprise should leave a mark.

## Setting the odds

Odds are your honest guess about the fiction, and they say nothing about what you want. Ask first what the world is like. A door in a quiet house is Likely to be locked, and a door in a house on fire is not. Then ask what the people in it want, since a guard who was paid to look away is Likely to look away, and last, ask what you have already written, because if an earlier line says the road is empty, a crowd on it is a Long shot.

If you still cannot decide, say Even and roll, and do not spend longer on the odds than on the question.

## Reading a "but"

The but is yours to write, and you write it from the question, by asking what would make the answer true and what that would cost. A yes, but pays a price, in time, in being seen, in something broken or in a debt left behind. A no, but is a door closing with a window beside it, so that you cannot do the thing, yet you notice something, or someone makes you an offer.

Keep the but to one sentence. If it needs a paragraph, you have started a scene, and the flow should run it.

## When to ask and when to decide

Ask when both answers would make a good story and you do not know which one you want to read. Decide instead when the story has already answered the question, as it has once you have written a door, and you need not roll to see whether the door exists. Decide, too, when one answer is dull and the other is not, by taking the interesting one and moving on. And decide when the question is about your own bearer or crew member, because what your character does is not a roll, although what the world does to them is.

Before you ask, rewrite an open question as a closed one. "Who is at the door?" is a question for a table or for a decision, whereas "Is it someone I know?" can be answered yes or no, so ask the closed one and let its answer prompt the open one.

Roll rarely. Three or four oracle questions in a scene is plenty, and beyond that you are reading the dice instead of the story.

## On paper

Keep a half page headed **Questions**, and write each question on one line, with the odds, the die and the answer.

> Is the keeper expecting us? Unlikely, 3: no, but.

Write the but beside it. A page of these lines is a record of the surprises you did not choose.

Do not erase a question because you did not like its answer. Cross an answer out only if you made an error in the roll, and when you do, say so.

## The Long Carry

The Long Carry does not ask a yes or no question of its own, which leaves the asking to the player, on the road, and the best questions are about what the bearers meet.

**The Road** has turned up a Ruin, a farm with the table laid for twelve, and the player wonders whether anyone is still in it.

> Is anyone still in the farm? Unlikely, the road has been quiet, and the place has been empty a long time. I roll a d6: 2. A plain yes would have been a 1, so this is the last yes face: yes, but.
>
> Yes, but. There is one old woman, and she does not say who she laid the table for. She has already set a place for the saint.

The answer came in against Unlikely odds, so the but is a heavy one, and the scene now has a person and a mystery in it.

## Cold Berth

In Cold Berth the questions come from the **Reveal** and the Truths. The crew writes down a hunch, and a likelihood oracle tests how well the hunch fits the ship.

During an Investigate scene, the player suspects the custodian of covering for someone.

> Is the custodian lying? Likely, it has refused every question about the empty berth. I roll a d6: 5. That is the first face of the no: no, but.
>
> No, but. It is telling the truth about the berth. What it does not say is how it knows.

This no holds a clue, so write the line in the notebook and keep the question open.

## Try it

Write three yes or no questions that your own flow is going to raise, and set the odds for each. Then think of one answer to each that you would be happy with and one that you would not. If you would be happy with both, you did not need to roll.
