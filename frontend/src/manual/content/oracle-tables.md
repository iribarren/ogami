# Oracle Tables

The table of random encounters at the back of a published adventure is an oracle table, so you have probably rolled on one before. A likelihood oracle answers a question that can be settled with yes or no, whereas an **oracle table** answers "what?", as in what is on the road, who is at the door or what went wrong in the night. You roll, you read a line, and the line gives you something to build on.

Almost everything a flow rolls is a table. The Long Carry has Road Risks, Strangers, Omens and Ruins, as well as the road deck that **The Road** draws the next Scene Type from, and Cold Berth has Ship trouble, Treason and three Truth tables. A table is a list of lines with a rule for choosing one.

## Three kinds

**Ranged.** You number the lines and roll the dice that cover them, so a table of eight lines takes a d8, and a table of twelve takes a d12, or two d6 added together if its lines run from 2 to 12. Every number must belong to a line, and no number may belong to two. Ranges are also how you make some lines rarer than others, by giving the common line the numbers 1 to 4 and the strange one a single number.

**Weighted.** Here some lines count more than once, and you write a number beside each line for its copies. Road Risks has a quiet road with three copies and six other lines with one copy each, nine in all. To use it, roll a die that reaches nine, count through the copies in order and stop on the line you land in. Weighted tables are easier to edit, because adding a copy grows the table by one and nothing needs renumbering.

**Nested.** A line can send you to another table. The traveller on Road Risks could, for instance, have ended with the words _roll Strangers_, although the real line sends you to a Stranger scene instead. You finish the first roll, follow the line and roll again. Use a nested table when one result needs a second answer before it is usable.

Even where a table does not nest, it is fine to roll again, on another table or on the same one, when a result makes no sense where you are or gives you too little to work with. Keep an eye on how many times you roll, though, because after too many rolls the first one can stop meaning anything.

## Matching a die to a table

Pick the smallest die that covers the number of lines, and decide what you will do with a roll above it.

| Lines | Die | If the roll is too high |
| ----- | --- | ----------------------- |
| 4     | d4  | none                    |
| 5     | d6  | roll again above 5      |
| 6     | d6  | none                    |
| 8     | d8  | none                    |
| 9     | d10 | roll again above 9      |
| 12    | d12 | none                    |

Rolling again is cleaner than bending the table, since a d10 that lands on 10 is then not a result at all, only a second roll. Write the rule at the head of the table, and you will not have to think about it again.

Two dice added together give a bell curve, with two d6 reaching 7 most often and 2 and 12 rarely. Use them when the middle lines should happen often and the ends should feel like events.

## Writing entries that prompt

An entry should hand you something to work with, without finishing the work for you.

- **Specific, not general.** "A boy with a bell and no one to ring it for" is a person, whereas "A child" is only a category.
- **Open, not closed.** "A tinker who will not look at the litter" makes you ask why, while "A hostile tinker" has already answered.
- **A hook in each.** Give the player something to take hold of, such as a name to give, a want to guess or a place to find, and aim for one thing they have to decide.
- **Quiet lines.** Give the entries that say nothing happens enough weight that about one roll in three comes up quiet, as Road Risks does with three of its nine copies, because without quiet rolls nothing stands out.
- **Cost where it counts.** A table that moves the game can say so on the line, as the hard frost on Road Risks also moves Winter by one.

Make the lines about the same size, because an entry that runs to a paragraph will take over every roll that lands near it.

## Testing a table

Before you play with a table, roll it ten times and read the results aloud, one after the other. If a line came up twice, that is fine, but if the same kind of line came up four times, the table is leaning. If a result made you want to roll again, find out why and fix the line. And if there is a line you hope you never roll, keep it, because it is the one that makes the others matter.

## On paper

Print or write a table in two columns, the number and then the line, and put the die at the top of the page. If the table has weights, write the numbers of each line as a range, so that you never have to count copies during play.

Note any effect beside its line in brackets, such as **(Winter +1)**, and do it when you write the table, so that you do not have to think of it again in play.

When you roll, read the line aloud and write one sentence of your own under it, since the line is the prompt and your sentence is the story.

:::in-ogami
A table is **Ranged (rolls dice)** or **Weighted (entries have a weight)**, chosen in **New table** on the **Oracles** page of a game system. A ranged table has a **Dice** field, such as 1d6, 2d6 or d%, and each entry has a **Min** and a **Max**. A weighted table has a **Weight** on each entry. In either, an entry can have a **Nested table**, a table of the same game system that is rolled next. Studio shows which numbers the ranges leave uncovered or cover twice.

In Play, the **Oracles** panel lists each table as a button, **Roll on** followed by its name. The result is recorded in the journal, and a nested table is followed to the end, all its steps shown.

Both shipped game systems use weighted tables only, and neither nests one.
:::

## The Long Carry

Road Risks is the table that **The Road** rolls at every World turn. It has nine copies in seven lines, so it takes a d10, and a roll of 10 is rolled again. Three copies are the quiet road, and the other six lines, with one copy each, are the risen ford, the hard frost, the bad foot, a traveller, smoke on a ridge and a thing that follows.

At one World turn the d10 comes up 7, which falls on the traveller.

> _A traveller falls into step beside you and starts to talk._ The line sends me to a Stranger scene, so I roll Strangers, a d8: 2.
>
> _A boy with a bell and no one to ring it for_. I write: "He keeps pace with the litter. When I ask who the bell is for, he says it is for whoever answers."

Road Risks made the first roll and the player nested the second one by hand, so the first table said _someone comes_ and the second said who.

## Cold Berth

Treason is rolled in an Investigate scene, at an optional step that asks whether someone is lying to you. It is a d8 table that mixes clues and threats, with four lines that give a Clue, two that raise Threat and two that do nothing.

When the player doubts someone and rolls Treason, the d8 gives a 1.

> _A falsified cryo log: a sleeper's wake time was edited, and the edit carries a crew key. Name the suspect._
>
> The entry hands me a job, not a name. I write: "Pell. The edit is dated three nights before we woke, and Pell was the only one awake." Clues goes up by one, and I start a Thread, "Who falsified the cryo log?"

## Try it

Write a table of eight entries for a place your flow visits. Give the quiet ones enough weight to come up about one roll in three, and give each of the others something a player has to decide.
