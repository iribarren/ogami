# Playtest and Share

You cannot see what is wrong with a flow by reading it. It reads well. The trouble shows when the dice are bad, a clock runs short, or a list is empty at the wrong moment. You have to play it, and then you have to watch it be played by someone else.

## Playtest alone first

Play the flow from Session Zero to the Epilogue, once, as if it were a game. Do not fix anything while you play. Write down what you notice, and fix it afterwards. If you stop to repair a rule in the middle, you will never learn how the rule felt.

Keep a log with three columns: the scene, what happened, and what bothered you. A line is enough.

## Then break it

One honest run tells you how the flow behaves on a fair day. The rest of the testing is for bad days.

- **Run the clock to its ends.** Start with the clock one box from full, and see the ending. Start with it empty, and count how many scenes it takes to reach the end. Does the flow last as long as the promise said?
- **Visit every ending.** If you cannot reach one by playing, no one will. Set the counters by hand and read the Epilogue.
- **Empty the lists.** What does a scene that picks a crew member do when the crew is gone? A flow should tell you what to do with an empty list.
- **Roll badly on purpose.** Take the worst result of each table in turn. Is there one you would never want to write about? Cut it or turn it into a question.
- **Roll the same result twice.** If one entry in eight is "nothing", then two nothings in a row will come up. Does the flow survive it?

## What to look for

- **A step that changes nothing.** If the scene reads as well without it, cut it.
- **A step you dread.** If you skip it every time, it was a chore.
- **A clock that ends the story too soon or never.** Count scenes, not boxes.
- **A tip that promises something the rules do not do.** The words on a card and the numbers on it must agree.
- **A moment where you had to stop and look something up.** Move it onto the card.
- **Prompts answered with a single word.** A prompt that gets one word is not asking a question.

When you find something, cut before you add. Most flows are better for being shorter.

## What the two flows found

Testing found real problems in both of these flows, and the fixes are in the cards you have.

- In The Long Carry, a failed river crossing wore down Wind, and the harm rule said a bearer with none left should take a Wound. The bands and the rule disagreed, so the bands were changed to agree with the rule.
- In Cold Berth, a Confront scene promised that it cost Nerve, but the band for a win took none. The tip was changed to say that only a confrontation that goes badly costs Nerve.
- In Cold Berth, the ship could take the same crew member twice. The fix is the step that says to cross the crew member off the list, so nobody is taken again.

None of them was visible on a first reading.

A played test line. I roll the Strangers table to try the scene.

> Strangers: _A priest of a smaller saint._ I play the scene through. The want comes easily: he asks to walk beside the litter and say a prayer over it. I give what he asks, and the Burden goes up by one.
>
> Log: the table gave a person with a want built in. A table entry like "A boy with a bell and no one to ring it for" gives less to work with. Both work. Keep both.

A second test line, from Cold Berth. I roll Treason with a suspect in mind.

> Treason: _Nothing. The one you suspect is only afraid._ I note it in the log. No Clue, and no harm. It is one entry in eight, and I want to see what two in a row feels like, so I roll again until I have it. It feels like a held breath, and that is right.

## Share

Give the flow to someone else, and then say nothing.

1. **Hand over the whole set.** The cards, the rules card and the endings card, the credits and the license. A flow someone cannot reproduce is a flow that stops at you.
2. **Tell them the promise.** One line, the premise. Do not explain the rules; the cards do that.
3. **Watch the first scene.** Do not help. Every question they ask is a card that is missing something.
4. **Ask three things afterwards.** Where did you stop? What did you skip? What do you remember?

Change the flow after the second person, not the first. One person's trouble is a person. Two people's trouble is the flow.

## On paper

On paper a flow travels alone. Everything it needs is in the pile you hand over, so check the pile before you do: every Scene Type has a card, every table is written out, the clocks and the endings are on the rules card, and the credits say where the ideas came from and what license the cards are under. Put your name on the rules card. Someone will have a question.

:::in-ogami
In Ogami a flow does not travel alone. It lives inside a GameSystem release, with the oracles, Scene Types, Trackers and sheet it uses, and it is shared by publishing that release. In Studio, **Validation** says whether the draft is valid and could be published, and **Publish** creates the next version. The **Flows** list marks one flow **Default**.

To test, make a campaign in Play: **New campaign** asks for the GameSystem and its **Flow**, and shows the flow's introduction. While you test, you can set a Tracker by hand, **Pause guidance** and play freely, and end a Phase early. The **Releases** tab keeps the history. A campaign stays on the release it started with until its player upgrades it.

## Try it

Write down the three worst things that could happen in your flow on a bad day. Play to each on purpose. Then give the flow to one person, say the premise, and watch the first scene.
