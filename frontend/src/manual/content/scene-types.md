# Scene Types

The arc says which Phases the story has, and each Phase is made of scenes. Every scene is an instance of a **Scene Type**, which is a reusable kind of scene that you write once and play many times.

## What a Scene Type is

A Scene Type is a recipe for one kind of scene. A camp, a river crossing, a conversation with a stranger and a search of a ruin are four such recipes. Each time the road sends you to a camp you follow the same recipe again, and because what goes into it is different each time, a new scene comes out.

A Scene Type has two parts of text and three parts of play. The **purpose** says what this kind of scene is for, and the **tips** are advice to yourself about running it. The **setup**, the **play** part and the **closing** hold the questions and rolls, in order.

## Purpose

The purpose is one sentence that says what the scene changes. "Cross water, and learn what the crossing costs" is a purpose, whereas "A river" is not, because it names a place rather than a job.

Once your Scene Types have purposes, you can use them to test the list. If two have the same purpose, they are one Scene Type, and you should merge them. If you cannot say what changes by the end of a scene, you have written down a place. And if one Scene Type does three jobs, split it, because a scene with three jobs has no single direction to take, so it ends without having moved the story anywhere.

## Tips

Tips are the craft notes you would give a friend who is about to run this scene. They are not rules. They hold what you tend to forget in the moment. With a stranger, the tip is to decide what they want before you decide what they are like, and with someone who has gone, it is to leave the person unexplained and describe what they left behind.

Write tips that change what you do. "Make it interesting" does nothing to a scene, but "Spend the night on people, not on scenery: who sleeps, who watches, who talks" changes it.

## Setup, play and closing

The three parts of play give every scene a shape.

- **Setup** frames the scene by answering who is there, where it happens and what is at stake. Keep it short, at two or three questions, so that you reach the middle of something before a description can get started.
- **Play** is where the story turns, so it holds the roll, the choice or the question that matters, and it is the part in which the dice and the oracles come out.
- **Closing** sends you out of the scene with one question about the aftermath, such as who is last to sleep, what you leave behind or what they said that you cannot unhear. It is how the scene stays in your memory.

If a scene feels flat, ask which part is missing. A scene with no setup has no ground under it, one with no play has no pressure, and one without a closing leaves nothing behind.

## Oracle shortcuts

A Scene Type can list the oracles that help it. A yes/no question helps any scene where you do not know how someone takes something, and a table of strangers helps the scene that meets one. The list is a menu rather than a requirement, and it sits next to the scene so that you do not have to look for those oracles.

## How many

You need fewer Scene Types than you think. A flow of four hours does not need twenty of them, so make the five or six that the story is built from, and add a seventh only when you notice yourself bending one to do a job it was not made for.

## On paper

Use one index card per Scene Type.

- **Front:** the name, the purpose, and the tips.
- **Back:** three short lists headed Setup, Play and Closing, each with its questions or its roll.

When you start a scene, put its card in front of you and read the front. Then answer the setup, do the play part and answer the closing, and put the card away. If a scene calls for a table, write the table on the card, or clip it to the card.

:::in-ogami
Open **Scene Types** in the GameSystem's left rail. **New Scene Type** opens the editor. Its fields are **Key**, **Name**, **Purpose** and **Tips**, and **Oracle shortcuts** is a checklist of the oracles that sit beside the scene when it is played. Under **Steps**, three lists named **Setup**, **Play** and **Closing** hold the steps, and **Add step** offers the kinds. Chapter 4 covers each kind.

A Scene Type belongs to the GameSystem, not to a flow. Several flows can use the same one.

## The Long Carry

The Road is built from five Scene Types, **Camp**, **Ford**, **Stranger**, **Ruin** and **Omen**, one for each thing the carry can meet, and their purposes read as a list of different jobs.

| Scene Type | Purpose                                                              |
| ---------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Camp       | Stop, rest and say what cannot be said while walking                 |
| Ford       | Cross water, and learn what the crossing costs                       |
| Stranger   | Meet someone who wants something from the road, or from you          |
| Ruin       | Find what earlier travellers left, and decide whether to take it     |
| Omen       | Let the road say something about the saint, and decide what it means |

No two of them do the same work. The Camp is the only place where the bearers recover, which is what makes it matter, and the Omen is the only one that rewrites the Sin. The other three each make a different demand, because a Ford costs time and breath, a Stranger asks and a Ruin tempts.

Look at the Ford card.

- **Tips:** _Pick the check by how you cross: Back to wade with the load, Feet to find the shallows. A risen ford is a Back check. Failing costs time and breath._
- **Setup:** What does the water look like, and who has crossed it before you?
- **Play:** Who carries the litter across, and how? A check, Back or Feet.
- **Closing:** What is left on the far bank, and who looks back at it?

The card holds two questions, a check and a closing question. In this round the table calls for a Ford, and the player plays it.

> The World turn rolls Road Risks: _The ford has risen. The water is up and the crossing cannot wait: it calls for a Back check. Winter closes by one more._ The next scene is a Ford, and Winter goes up.
>
> Setup. "The water is the colour of weak tea and fast. The last party to cross left a rope tied to a willow. It frays at the knot."
>
> Play. Back is 12. I roll a d20 and get 15, over the trait, so the crossing fails. Winter goes up by one more and the bearer who waded loses d6 Wind. "The litter tilts. We save it, but I lose the rope and my breath."
>
> Closing. "On the far bank: a boot. Mine. Ansel looks back at it longer than I like."

The table did not describe the ford, since all it said was that the ford had risen. It was the Scene Type that asked the questions that made the water real, and the check that made the crossing cost something.

## Cold Berth

The Ship loop has four Scene Types, **Investigate**, **Repair**, **Confront** and **Hide**, and each of them is a job the crew can take on.

| Scene Type  | Purpose                                                               |
| ----------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Investigate | Look closely at one part of the ship, and learn what is wrong with it |
| Repair      | Fix something that must work, and pay for what it takes               |
| Confront    | Stand in front of the thing or person you have been avoiding          |
| Hide        | Get out of sight and wait for it to pass                              |

They differ in what each one does to Threat, Clues and the crew's Nerve, so each of the four is a different bargain. An Investigate scene that goes well adds a Clue, a Repair that goes well lowers Threat, a Confront puts Nerve at risk to gain a Clue, and a Hide can give Nerve back.

Every one of these Scene Types begins in the same way, with a setup that picks a compartment you named in Session Zero and asks what is out of place, what has failed, what waits there or what makes it a place to hide. We made the setup shared on purpose, because it ties every job to a place on the ship, so the ship is never a backdrop.

This is the Repair card.

- **Tips:** _Open by picking a compartment. A repair buys time: it lowers Threat. A failed one costs Nerve. Harm takes Nerve first; with no Nerve left, mark an Injury._
- **Setup:** Which compartment needs you? What has failed in it?
- **Play:** How do you fix it? A check, on the Station you choose.
- **Closing:** How long will the repair hold, and who knows it?

In this round the table calls for a Repair.

> The World turn rolls Ship trouble: _Something you were relying on fails._ The next scene is a Repair.
>
> Setup. Compartments: the Wake Bay, the Drive Spine, the Cold Hold. "The scrubbers in the Cold Hold have stopped. The fans spin, but they move nothing."
>
> Play. My crew member's Hands are a d8, and the Difficulty is the default, 3. I roll a 2. It falls short, which changes nothing: the fans keep spinning and the air stays bad.
>
> Closing. "It holds for an hour, maybe. Only the custodian knows that I tried."

The result of a scene does not have to be a number. Here the check changed no clock, but the closing question gave the next scene something to be about.

## Try it

List the Scene Types your story needs, and for each one write the purpose in one sentence and the three parts as one line each. Then read the purposes aloud, one after the other, and if any two sound the same, merge them.
