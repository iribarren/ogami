# Scene Types

The arc says which Phases the story has. A Phase is made of scenes, and each scene is an instance of a **Scene Type**: a reusable kind of scene that you write once and play many times.

## What a Scene Type is

A Scene Type is a recipe for one kind of scene. A camp, a river crossing, a conversation with a stranger and a search of a ruin are four recipes. Each time the road sends you to a camp, you cook the same recipe with different ingredients, and a new scene comes out.

A Scene Type has three parts of text and three parts of play:

- A **purpose**: what this kind of scene is for.
- **Tips**: advice to yourself about running it.
- A **setup**, a **play** part and a **closing**: the questions and rolls, in order.

## Purpose

The purpose is one sentence that says what the scene changes. "Cross water, and learn what the crossing costs" is a purpose. "A river" is not: it names a place and not a job.

Use the purpose to test your list of Scene Types.

- If two have the same purpose, they are one Scene Type. Merge them.
- If you cannot say what changes by the end of a scene, it is a place, not a scene.
- If one Scene Type does three jobs, split it. A scene that can go anywhere never goes anywhere.

## Tips

Tips are the craft notes you would give a friend who is about to run this scene. They are not rules. They are the thing you tend to forget in the moment: decide what a stranger wants before you decide what they are like; do not explain the person who left, describe what they left behind.

Write tips that change what you do. "Make it interesting" does nothing. "Spend the night on people, not on scenery: who sleeps, who watches, who talks" changes the scene.

## Setup, play and closing

The three parts give every scene a shape.

- **Setup** frames the scene. It answers who, where and what is at stake. Keep it short: two or three questions. It should leave you in the middle of something, not at the start of a description.
- **Play** is where the story turns. It holds the roll, the choice or the question that matters. This part has the dice, the oracle and the table.
- **Closing** sends you out of the scene. It is one question about the aftermath: who is last to sleep, what do you leave behind, what did they say that you cannot unhear. It is how the scene stays in your memory.

If a scene feels flat, ask which part is missing. A scene with no setup has no ground. A scene with no play has no pressure. A scene with no closing leaves nothing behind.

## Oracle shortcuts

A Scene Type can list the oracles that help it: a yes/no question for any scene where you do not know how someone takes something, a table of strangers for the scene that meets one. These are a menu, not a requirement. They sit next to the scene so you do not have to look for them.

## How many

Fewer than you think. A flow of four hours does not need twenty Scene Types. Make the five or six that the story is built from, and add a seventh only when you notice yourself bending one to do a job it was not made for.

## On paper

Use one index card per Scene Type.

- **Front:** the name, the purpose, and the tips.
- **Back:** three short lists headed Setup, Play and Closing, each with its questions or its roll.

When you start a scene, put its card in front of you and read the front. Answer the setup. Do the play part. Answer the closing, and put the card away.

If a scene calls for a table, write it on the card, or clip it to the card.

:::in-ogami
Open **Scene Types** in the GameSystem's left rail. **New Scene Type** opens the editor. Its fields are **Key**, **Name**, **Purpose** and **Tips**, and **Oracle shortcuts** is a checklist of the oracles that sit beside the scene when it is played. Under **Steps**, three lists named **Setup**, **Play** and **Closing** hold the steps, and **Add step** offers the kinds. Chapter 4 covers each kind.

A Scene Type belongs to the GameSystem, not to a flow. Several flows can use the same one.

## The Long Carry

The Road is built from five Scene Types, one for each thing the carry can meet: **Camp**, **Ford**, **Stranger**, **Ruin** and **Omen**. Their purposes are a list of different jobs.

| Scene Type | Purpose                                                              |
| ---------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Camp       | Stop, rest and say what cannot be said while walking                 |
| Ford       | Cross water, and learn what the crossing costs                       |
| Stranger   | Meet someone who wants something from the road, or from you          |
| Ruin       | Find what earlier travellers left, and decide whether to take it     |
| Omen       | Let the road say something about the saint, and decide what it means |

No two do the same work. The Camp is the only place the bearers recover, which makes it matter. The Omen is the only one that rewrites the Sin. A Ford costs time and breath. A Stranger asks and a Ruin tempts.

Look at the Ford card.

- **Tips:** _Pick the check by how you cross: Back to wade with the load, Feet to find the shallows. A risen ford is a Back check. Failing costs time and breath._
- **Setup:** What does the water look like, and who has crossed it before you?
- **Play:** Who carries the litter across, and how? A check, Back or Feet.
- **Closing:** What is left on the far bank, and who looks back at it?

Two questions, a check and a closing question. A played Ford, called by the table:

> The world turn rolls Road Risks: _The ford has risen. The water is up and the crossing cannot wait: it calls for a Back check. Winter closes by one more._ The next scene is a Ford, and Winter goes up.
>
> Setup. "The water is the colour of weak tea and fast. The last party to cross left a rope tied to a willow. It frays at the knot."
>
> Play. Back is 12. I roll a d20: 15, over the trait, so the crossing fails. Winter goes up by one more and the bearer who waded loses d6 Wind. "The litter tilts. We save it, but I lose the rope and my breath."
>
> Closing. "On the far bank: a boot. Mine. Ansel looks back at it longer than I like."

The table did not describe the ford. It only said it had risen. The Scene Type asked the questions that made the water real, and the check made it cost something.

## Cold Berth

The Ship loop has four Scene Types, and they are jobs the crew can take on: **Investigate**, **Repair**, **Confront** and **Hide**.

| Scene Type  | Purpose                                                               |
| ----------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Investigate | Look closely at one part of the ship, and learn what is wrong with it |
| Repair      | Fix something that must work, and pay for what it takes               |
| Confront    | Stand in front of the thing or person you have been avoiding          |
| Hide        | Get out of sight and wait for it to pass                              |

They differ in what each does to the two clocks. An Investigate scene that goes well adds a Clue. A Repair that goes well lowers Threat. A Confront risks the crew's Nerve for a Clue. A Hide can give Nerve back. Four scenes, four different bargains.

Every one of these Scene Types begins the same way: the setup picks a compartment you named in Session Zero, and asks what is out of place, what has failed or what waits there. The shared setup is deliberate. It ties every job to a place on the ship, so the ship is never a backdrop.

The Repair card:

- **Tips:** _Open by picking a compartment. A repair buys time: it lowers Threat. A failed one costs breath. Harm takes Nerve first; with no Nerve left, mark an Injury._
- **Setup:** Which compartment needs you? What has failed in it?
- **Play:** How do you fix it? A check, on the Station you choose.
- **Closing:** How long will the repair hold, and who knows it?

A played Repair, called by the table:

> The world turn rolls Ship trouble: _Something you were relying on fails._ The next scene is a Repair.
>
> Setup. Compartments: the Wake Bay, the Drive Spine, the Cold Hold. "The scrubbers in the Cold Hold have stopped. The fans spin, but they move nothing."
>
> Play. My crew member's Hands are a d8, and the Difficulty is the default, 3. I roll a 2. It falls short, which changes nothing: the fans keep spinning and the air stays bad.
>
> Closing. "It holds for an hour, maybe. Only the custodian knows that I tried."

A scene's result does not have to be a number. Here the check changed no clock, but the closing question gave the next scene something to be about.

## Try it

List the Scene Types your story needs. For each, write the purpose in one sentence and the three parts as one line each. Then read the purposes aloud, one after the other. If any two sound the same, merge them.
