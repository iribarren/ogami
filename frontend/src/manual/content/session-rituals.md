# Session Rituals

A story is not a sitting. The Long Carry takes three or four sittings; Cold Berth takes two or three. Between them you go away, and you come back with a head full of other things.

A sitting has two weak points. You start it cold: you have forgotten who is awake and what Winter stood at. And you end it badly: you stop because the hour is late, in the middle of a scene, with nothing to start from next time. A ritual at each end fixes both.

## Hooks

A **hook** is a short list of steps that the flow runs at a boundary. The flow has seven boundaries.

| Hook            | When it runs                         | What it is good for                             |
| --------------- | ------------------------------------ | ----------------------------------------------- |
| Session opening | When you sit down                    | Remembering, and re-reading the clocks          |
| Session closing | When you stand up                    | Saying where you stopped, and what is open      |
| Phase opening   | When a Phase begins                  | Naming what this stretch of the story is for    |
| Phase closing   | When a Phase ends                    | Marking what changed                            |
| Scene opening   | At the start of every scene          | A question every scene of the Phase should ask  |
| Scene closing   | At the end of every scene            | A check, a tick, a question about the aftermath |
| World turn      | Between scenes, after the scene ends | Clocks and events                               |

You have met two already. The check that ends a loop is a scene closing, and the world turn is the heart of the Pressure chapter. The other five are about rhythm, and they are where a session ritual lives.

## Opening a sitting

An opening has three jobs.

- **Remember.** Read the last note you wrote, aloud if you can. Your own words bring the mood back.
- **Re-read the pressure.** Look at every clock and counter. Read the open Threads. A clock you have not looked at today is a clock you will ignore.
- **Start in motion.** Ask one question that puts you in the middle of something. "Where did you stop, and who is awake?" is better than a summary.

Keep an opening to three steps. If it takes longer than a minute you will start skipping it.

## Closing a sitting

A closing has two jobs.

- **Stop between scenes.** A scene left half-played is a scene you will not remember how to finish. If you must stop, write one line saying where you were.
- **Leave a door open.** Write the question you will start with next time, and leave it open. A sitting that ends with every question answered is a sitting that has nothing to come back to.

## What the two flows do

Neither flow has a session opening, a session closing, a phase hook or a scene opening. They are written as one long run, and you decide when to stop. They use only two of the seven: the scene closing, which reads the clock, and the world turn.

That is a choice, and it is not the only good one. Sittings are short, and a reminder you do not need is a step you resent. If your own flow runs for many sittings, or the clocks are easy to forget, add a ritual.

The two examples below were written for this chapter. They are **not in the flows as built**. They show what a ritual looks like.

**A session opening for The Long Carry.**

1. Prompt: "Where did the bearers stop last night, and who is awake?"
2. Table: roll the Omens table. It is only for colour here; it changes no number.
3. Prompt: "Which Sin do you wake believing?"

**A session closing for Cold Berth.**

1. Prompt: "What do you do before the lights go down?"
2. Prompt: "Which question will you start with next time?" Write it at the foot of the Threads page.

Neither has an effect. A ritual does not have to move a clock. It can only make the start and the end of a sitting feel like a start and an end.

A played opening. I sit down to the third sitting of The Long Carry with Winter at 4.

> Step 1. "The litter is propped against the wall of a byre. Ansel keeps the watch. He has not slept."
>
> Step 2. I roll the Omens table: _A smell of roses where the ground is bare._ "It is the middle of the night, and the straw smells of roses."
>
> Step 3. "The last Sin I wrote was that the saint betrayed a friend. In the dark I want it to be a kindness. I leave it as it is."

The table did not change the story. It gave the first minute a strangeness, and the player found something to write in it.

## On paper

Keep one card in the front of the notebook, headed **Sitting**.

**When I sit down**

1. Read the last note aloud.
2. Look at every clock and counter. Say the numbers.
3. Read the open Threads.
4. Ask the opening question, and answer it.

**When I stand up**

1. Finish the scene, or write one line saying where it stopped.
2. Write the question to start with next time.
3. Close every Thread that was answered.

Treat the card as a gate. You do not start without reading it, and you do not leave without writing the last line.

In Studio, every Phase in the flow editor's outline lists seven hooks: **Session opening**, **Session closing**, **Phase opening**, **Phase closing**, **Scene opening**, **Scene closing** and **World turn**, each with the number of its steps. Select one to edit its steps as you would a Scene Type's.

In Play, **Start session** runs the session opening of the Phase you are in before the first scene pick. A Phase opening runs before the first scene of a Phase, and a world turn runs after each scene. At the scene pick, **End session** runs the session closing. A session closing runs only in the Phase where the session's opening ran. A hook is recorded in the journal as its own entry, headed **Session opening**, **Session closing**, **Phase opening**, **Phase closing** or **World turn**.
