# Session Rituals

The Long Carry takes three or four sessions to play and Cold Berth two or three, so a story is longer than one sitting at the table. Between sessions you go away, and you come back with a head full of other things.

A television series has the same trouble with its audience, who have had a week to forget the last episode, and that is why an episode so often opens with a recap of what happened before and ends on a question you want answered. A session has the same two weak points. You start it cold, having forgotten who is awake and what Winter stood at, and you end it badly, because you stop when the hour is late, in the middle of a scene, with nothing to start from next time. A ritual at each end of the session fixes both.

## Hooks

A **hook** is a short list of steps that the flow runs at a boundary, the moment when one thing ends and the next begins, and the flow has seven of these boundaries.

| Hook            | When it runs                         | What it is good for                             |
| --------------- | ------------------------------------ | ----------------------------------------------- |
| Session opening | When you sit down                    | Remembering, and re-reading the clocks          |
| Session closing | When you stand up                    | Saying where you stopped, and what is open      |
| Phase opening   | When a Phase begins                  | Naming what this stretch of the story is for    |
| Phase closing   | When a Phase ends                    | Marking what changed                            |
| Scene opening   | At the start of every scene          | A question every scene of the Phase should ask  |
| Scene closing   | At the end of every scene            | A check, a tick, a question about the aftermath |
| World turn      | Between scenes, after the scene ends | Clocks and events                               |

You have met two of them already, since the closing check that ends a loop is a scene closing, and the World turn is at the centre of Chapter 7. The other five are about rhythm, and they are where a session ritual lives.

## Opening a session

An opening has three jobs. It starts by helping you **remember**, so read the last note you wrote, aloud if you can, because your own words bring the mood back. Once the mood is back, **re-read the pressure** by looking at every clock and counter and reading the open Threads, since a clock you have not looked at today is a clock you will ignore. Then **start in motion**, with one question that puts you in the middle of something. "Where did you stop, and who is awake?" is better than a summary.

Keep an opening to three steps, because if it takes longer than a minute you will start skipping it.

## Closing a session

At the other end of the session, **stop between scenes**, because a scene left half-played is a scene you will not remember how to finish. If you must stop in the middle of one, write one line saying where you were. Before you stand up, **leave a door open** by writing the question you will start with next time and leaving it unanswered, since a session that ends with every question answered has nothing to come back to.

## What the two flows do

Neither flow has a session opening, a session closing, a phase hook or a scene opening. Both are written as one long run that you stop when you decide to, and of the seven hooks they use only two. One is the scene closing, which reads the clock, and the other is the World turn.

That is a choice, and not the only good one, made because sessions are short and a reminder you do not need is a step you resent. If your own flow runs for many sessions, or its clocks are easy to forget, add a ritual.

The two examples below were written for this chapter, and they are **not in the flows as built**. They are here to show what a ritual looks like.

**A session opening for The Long Carry.**

1. Prompt: "Where did the bearers stop last night, and who is awake?"
2. Table: roll the Omens table. It is only for colour here; it changes no number.
3. Prompt: "Which Sin do you wake believing?"

**A session closing for Cold Berth.**

1. Prompt: "What do you do before the lights go down?"
2. Prompt: "Which question will you start with next time?" Write it at the foot of the Threads page.

Neither of them has an effect. A ritual does not have to move a clock, and it can do no more than make the start and the end of a session feel like a start and an end.

The player sits down to the third session of The Long Carry with Winter at 4 and plays the opening above.

> Step 1. "The litter is propped against the wall of a byre. Ansel keeps the watch. He has not slept."
>
> Step 2. I roll the Omens table and it gives _A smell of roses where the ground is bare_. "It is the middle of the night, and the straw smells of roses."
>
> Step 3. "The last Sin I wrote was that the saint betrayed a friend. In the dark I want it to be a kindness. I leave it as it is."

The table changed nothing in the story, but it gave the first minute a strangeness, and the player found something to write in it.

## On paper

Head an index card, or a page of your notebook, with the word **Session**, and keep it at the front of the notebook.

**When I sit down**

1. Read the last note aloud.
2. Look at every clock and counter. Say the numbers.
3. Read the open Threads.
4. Ask the opening question, and answer it.

**When I stand up**

1. Finish the scene, or write one line saying where it stopped.
2. Write the question to start with next time.
3. Close every Thread that was answered.

Treat whichever you use as a gate, which means that you do not start without reading it, and you do not leave without writing the last line.
