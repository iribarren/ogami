# Steps

A Scene Type is a list of **Steps**, and each step is one thing the scene asks of you, such as answering a question, making a choice, rolling on a table, making a check or reading a clock.

If a Scene Type is the recipe for a kind of scene, its steps are the instructions, the lines you follow one at a time. That is how they move the story forward, because you do not have to see the whole scene before you start, only answer the step in front of you, and each answer, roll or choice gives the next step something to work with. A recipe still leaves the cooking to you, though, and most steps ask rather than tell, which is why the steps are where the flow and the story meet.

## The kinds of step

You do not need to know every kind to design a good flow, because most steps are one of these.

| Kind          | What it asks of you                                                        |
| ------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Prompt**    | A question. You answer it in writing                                       |
| **Choice**    | A short list of options. You pick one, and each can cost or give something |
| **Oracle**    | A yes/no question and how likely you think the answer is. The dice answer  |
| **Table**     | A roll on an oracle table. The result is the answer                        |
| **Roll**      | A die, read against bands: low, middle, high                               |
| **Check**     | A roll against a trait, with an outcome by band                            |
| **Condition** | Nothing. It reads a clock or a counter and sends you on                    |
| **Pick**      | A draw from your lists of people, places and open questions                |

There are two more kinds, Character and Shop, which build a character and its kit. They belong to the rules of a GameSystem rather than to the craft of a flow.

## Prompts: write questions, not answers

The prompt is the step you will use most, and it is also where first flows go wrong. The temptation is to write the scene for the player, with a line such as "The ford is wide and cold, and a rope hangs from a willow." Writing it that way makes you the game master and leaves the player with nothing to do, so write the question instead, and let the answer be yours when you play.

| Instead of                | Ask                                                               |
| ------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------- |
| The ford is wide and cold | What does the water look like, and who has crossed it before you? |
| A stranger asks for help  | What do they ask of you, and what do they offer?                  |
| The room is a mess        | What is out of place?                                             |

A good question does four things.

- **It asks for a thing you can see.** "What does the water look like" gets a picture, whereas "How do you feel" gets a word.
- **It asks for a person.** "Who has crossed it before you" brings in someone with a past.
- **It cannot be answered with yes or no.** A yes/no question is for the oracle.
- **It leaves the answer open.** If you already know what you want to hear, you are not asking.

Closing questions ask about the aftermath, and they are the kind of question you can only ask once the story has happened, such as "What is left on the far bank, and who looks back at it?"

## Choices

A choice step offers a few options, and each option can have a cost. Do not offer a choice in which one option is always best. "Give what they ask / Trade fairly / Refuse and walk on" works as a choice because its options answer the stranger in three different ways, and only one of them makes the load heavier. Write the options in the active voice, and keep them to three or four.

## Branches

A step does not always lead to the next one, because a **branch** can send the scene somewhere else, depending on what happened. A table sends you on according to the entry you rolled, and a condition according to a band, with "Contained", for example, going one way and "Boiling over" another. A pick sends you one way when it finds something and another way when the list is empty.

Branches are how a flow reacts without a game master. Use them where the story should split and nowhere else, since a scene that branches at every step turns into a maze that nobody remembers afterwards.

## Effects

An **effect** is what a step does to your book-keeping once the step is done. It can add to a clock, set a Fact, create a person or a place, open a question, force the next scene or end a Phase. A flow without effects would ask you questions and then carry on as if you had not answered them, so your answers would only seem to matter. Effects keep the flow honest about this, because each one carries an answer into the rest of the game. Attach one wherever the story changes, so that the stranger you meet becomes a name in the notebook, the Omen you read becomes the Sin you now believe and the trouble you roll adds to the clock.

## Mandatory and suggested

A step is either a gate or a suggestion. A **mandatory** step must be answered before the scene goes on, while a **suggested** step can be skipped. Make a step mandatory when later steps depend on its answer, and leave every other step suggested, so that the player keeps some freedom.

## On paper

Write each step as one line on its Scene Type's index card or notebook page, in this form:

> **Kind** · the question or the options · what it changes

For example, "Prompt · What do they ask of you, and what do they offer? · nothing", or "Choice · Give / Trade / Refuse · Give: +1 Burden".

When a step changes the book-keeping, write the change in the margin as a sum, such as "+1 Winter", "Add Pell to the manifest" or "Write the Sin". Do it the moment you finish the step, because if you wait until the end of the scene you will forget one.

:::in-ogami
Under a Scene Type's **Steps**, **Add step** offers the kinds prompt, character, roll, condition, oracle, table, choice, pick, check and shop. Every step has a **Key**, a **Title** and a **Tip**, and the kinds that ask a question also have a **Prompt**. The **Requirement** switch is set to **Mandatory** or **Suggested**. **Next** sends the step somewhere else, and its menu also offers a new step on the spot. A table step has a **Branch** for each entry, while a choice has **Options**, each with a **Label**. A condition has bands, which you add with **Add band**. At the bottom of a step, **Effects** has **Add effect**.

In the text of a step you can write `{picked}` for the names a pick chose, and `{fact:sin}` for a Fact, using the key of its slot. In an effect that makes a person, a place or a question, `{answer}` stands for what the player just wrote.
:::

## The Long Carry

The Stranger scene is a good small example. These are its steps, in order.

1. **Setup, prompt.** _Who is on the road ahead?_ It is mandatory, and it creates a person in the notebook from the answer.
2. **Play, prompt.** _What do they ask of you, and what do they offer?_
3. **Play, choice.** _How do you answer them?_ Give what they ask (Burden goes up by one), Trade fairly, or Refuse and walk on.
4. **Closing, prompt.** _What do you say about them after they are gone?_

The scene has a Strangers table as an oracle shortcut, for when you have no one in mind. The road deck has just given a Stranger, and the player starts on its setup.

> Setup. I have no one in mind, so I roll the Strangers table, which gives _A widow carrying her husband's boots_, and I write: "Maud. She walks up the track with a pair of boots held to her chest like a child." Maud goes into the notebook as a person.
>
> Play. "She wants us to take the boots to the shrine. She offers a coin and the name of a good ford." The choice asks _How do you answer them?_ Giving more than I can spare is the heavy option. I take the boots, and Burden goes up by one.
>
> Closing. "I say nothing about her. I check the ties on the litter twice."

Count the steps that did something. The table gave a person, the prompt gave her a want, the choice put a price on that want and the effect wrote the price on the Burden counter, so the shape of the scene came from the flow even though every word of it was the player's.

## Cold Berth

The Investigate scene has a branch at its first step. These are its steps.

1. **Setup, pick.** _Which compartment do you go to?_ A mandatory pick of a compartment. If it finds one, the scene asks _What is out of place in the place you chose?_ If none is left, it asks you to name a new compartment, and the ship has one more from then on.
2. **Play, check.** _How do you look, and what are you looking for?_
3. **Play, table.** _Is someone lying to you?_ The Treason table. Optional.
4. **Closing, prompt.** _What do you take away from the room, and what do you leave in it?_

The pick is mandatory because everything after it depends on the place, while the Treason step is suggested because not every Investigate has a person in it. The player has just chosen an Investigate, and this time rolls the Treason table as well.

> Setup. The ship offers the Cold Hold. "What is out of place? A row of sleepers, and a fourth that is not where the plan says."
>
> Play. I roll the Treason table: _A falsified cryo log: a sleeper's wake time was edited, and the edit carries a crew key. Name the suspect._ Clues go up by one, and a question opens in the notebook: _Who falsified the cryo log?_
>
> I name a suspect. "The edit was made with Pell's key. Pell has been asleep for eleven years." Either Pell lied about that, or someone has Pell's key.

The effect on the table entry did the book-keeping, adding a Clue and a new question to carry, but the suspect was still the player's to name. That is the use of an oracle table in a step, where the table supplies what was found and you supply the person.

## Try it

Take one of your Scene Types and write its steps as lines on its card or page. Check that each prompt is a question, and ask of each step whether it changes anything. If a step changes nothing and the scene reads as well without it, cut it.
