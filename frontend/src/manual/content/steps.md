# Steps

A Scene Type is a list of **Steps**. A step is one thing the scene asks of you: answer a question, make a choice, roll on a table, make a check, or read a clock. Steps are where the flow touches the story, and they are the part of the flow you will write most.

## The kinds of step

You do not need to know every kind to design a good flow. Most steps are one of these.

| Kind          | What it asks of you                                                        |
| ------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Prompt**    | A question. You answer it in writing                                       |
| **Choice**    | A short list of options. You pick one, and each can cost or give something |
| **Oracle**    | A yes/no question and how likely you think the answer is. The dice answer  |
| **Table**     | A roll on an oracle table. The result is the answer                        |
| **Roll**      | A die, read against bands: low, middle, high                               |
| **Check**     | A roll against a trait, with an outcome by band                            |
| **Condition** | Nothing. It reads a clock or a counter and sends you on                    |
| **Pick**      | A draw from your lists of people, places and open questions                |

Two more, Character and Shop, build a character and its kit. They belong to the rules of a GameSystem and not to the craft of a flow.

## Prompts: write questions, not answers

A prompt is the step you will use most, and it is where first flows go wrong. The temptation is to write the scene for the player: "The ford is wide and cold, and a rope hangs from a willow." That is you being the game master, and the player has nothing to do.

Write the question, and let the answer be yours at the table.

| Instead of                | Ask                                                               |
| ------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------- |
| The ford is wide and cold | What does the water look like, and who has crossed it before you? |
| A stranger asks for help  | What do they ask of you, and what do they offer?                  |
| The room is a mess        | What is out of place?                                             |

A good question has four habits.

- **It asks for a thing you can see.** "What does the water look like" gets a picture. "How do you feel" gets a word.
- **It asks for a person.** "Who has crossed it before you" brings in someone with a past.
- **It cannot be answered with yes or no.** A yes/no question is for the oracle.
- **It leaves the answer open.** If you already know what you want to hear, you are not asking.

Closing questions ask about the aftermath: "What is left on the far bank, and who looks back at it?" They are the kind of question you only ask once the story has happened.

## Choices

A choice step offers a few options, and each option can have a cost. Do not offer a choice where one option is always best. "Give what they ask / Trade fairly / Refuse and walk on" is a choice because the options answer the stranger in three different ways, and only one of them makes the load heavier.

Write options in the active voice, and keep them to three or four.

## Branches

A step does not always lead to the next one. A **branch** sends the scene somewhere else, depending on what happened.

- A table sends you according to the entry you rolled.
- A condition sends you according to a band: "Contained" goes one way, "Boiling over" goes another.
- A pick sends you one way when it finds something and another when the list is empty.

Branches are how a flow reacts without a game master. Use them where the story should split, and nowhere else. A scene that branches at every step is a maze, and nobody remembers a maze.

## Effects

An **effect** is what a step does to your book-keeping when it is done: it adds to a clock, sets a Fact, creates a person or a place, opens a question, forces the next scene or ends a Phase. Effects keep the flow honest, because they make an answer change the rest of the game.

Attach an effect where the story changes: the stranger you meet becomes a name in the notebook, the Omen you read becomes the Sin you now believe, the trouble you roll adds to the clock.

## Mandatory and suggested

A step is either a gate or a suggestion. A **mandatory** step must be answered before the scene goes on. A **suggested** step can be skipped.

Make a step mandatory when later steps depend on its answer. Everything else is suggested, so the player has room.

## On paper

Write each step as one line on its Scene Type card, in this form:

> **Kind** · the question or the options · what it changes

For example: "Prompt · What do they ask of you, and what do they offer? · nothing". "Choice · Give / Trade / Refuse · Give: +1 Burden".

When a step changes the book-keeping, write the change in the margin as a sum: "+1 Winter", "Add Pell to the manifest", "Write the Sin". Do it the moment you finish the step. If you wait until the end of the scene you will forget one.

:::in-ogami
Under a Scene Type's **Steps**, **Add step** offers the kinds: prompt, character, roll, condition, oracle, table, choice, pick, check and shop. Every step has a **Key**, a **Title** and a **Tip**, and a **Prompt** for the kinds that ask a question. The **Requirement** switch is **Mandatory** or **Suggested**. **Next** sends the step somewhere else, and the menu offers a new step on the spot. A table step has a **Branch** for each entry, and a choice has **Options**, each with a **Label**. A condition has bands, with **Add band**. **Effects** at the bottom of a step has **Add effect**.

In the text of a step you can write `{picked}` for the names a pick chose, and `{fact:sin}` for a Fact, using the key of its slot. In an effect that makes a person, a place or a question, `{answer}` stands for what the player just wrote.
:::

## The Long Carry

The Stranger scene is a good small example. Its steps, in order:

1. **Setup, prompt.** _Who is on the road ahead?_ It is mandatory, and it creates a person in the notebook from the answer.
2. **Play, prompt.** _What do they ask of you, and what do they offer?_
3. **Play, choice.** _How do you answer them?_ Give what they ask (Burden goes up by one), Trade fairly, or Refuse and walk on.
4. **Closing, prompt.** _What do you say about them after they are gone?_

The scene has a Strangers table as an oracle shortcut, for when you have no one in mind. A played Stranger:

> Setup. I have no one in mind, so I roll the Strangers table: _A widow carrying her husband's boots._ I write: "Maud. She walks up the track with a pair of boots held to her chest like a child." Maud goes into the notebook as a person.
>
> Play. "She wants us to take the boots to the shrine. She offers a coin and the name of a good ford." The choice: _How do you answer them?_ Giving more than I can spare is the heavy option. I take the boots. Burden goes up by one.
>
> Closing. "I say nothing about her. I check the ties on the litter twice."

Count the steps that did something. The table gave a person, the prompt gave her a want, the choice put a price on it and the effect wrote the price on the Burden counter. The player invented the words, and the flow supplied the shape.

## Cold Berth

The Investigate scene has a branch at its first step. Its steps:

1. **Setup, pick.** _Which compartment do you go to?_ A mandatory pick of a compartment. If it finds one, the scene asks _What is out of place in the place you chose?_ If none is left, it asks you to name a new compartment, and the ship has one more from then on.
2. **Play, check.** _How do you look, and what are you looking for?_
3. **Play, table.** _Is someone lying to you?_ The Treason table. Optional.
4. **Closing, prompt.** _What do you take away from the room, and what do you leave in it?_

The pick is mandatory because everything after it depends on the place. The Treason step is suggested, because not every Investigate has a person in it. A played Investigate, with the table rolled:

> Setup. The ship offers the Cold Hold. "What is out of place? A row of sleepers, and a fourth that is not where the plan says."
>
> Play. I roll the Treason table: _A falsified cryo log: a sleeper's wake time was edited, and the edit carries a crew key. Name the suspect._ Clues go up by one, and a question opens in the notebook: _Who falsified the cryo log?_
>
> I name a suspect. "The edit was made with Pell's key. Pell has been asleep for eleven years." Either Pell lied about that, or someone has Pell's key.

The effect on the table entry did the book-keeping: a Clue, and a new question to carry. The suspect was the player's to name. This is the use of an oracle table in a step: it supplies the fact, you supply the person.

## Try it

Take one of your Scene Types. Write its steps as lines on the card. For each prompt, check that it is a question. For each step, ask whether it changes anything. If a step changes nothing and the scene reads as well without it, cut it.
