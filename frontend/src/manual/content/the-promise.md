# The Promise

Every flow starts with a promise: what this story is about, how it feels, and how long it lasts. You make it before you design a single scene. Everything below it exists to keep it.

## The three jobs of a game master

At a table with a game master, one person does three jobs for everyone else.

- **Pacing.** They know when to rest the players, when to press them and when the story is over.
- **Surprise.** They know things the players do not, and they bring them in at the right moment.
- **Memory.** They remember the names, the debts, the open questions and the promises made three sessions ago.

Alone, you have no one to do these jobs. You also cannot surprise yourself by deciding something, because you already know it. A **NarrativeFlow** is how you hire the jobs out to paper.

| Job      | What the flow does about it                                                                                                        |
| -------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Pacing   | Phases give the story a shape. Clocks make time pass. A closing question ends each scene                                           |
| Surprise | Oracle tables and shuffled decks answer when you would only answer yourself. A Truth you have not seen yet pulls the story forward |
| Memory   | Trackers count, lists hold names, Facts record what you decided. The notebook holds all of it                                      |

Each later chapter builds one of these. The promise is the first piece, because it tells the rest what to protect.

## The premise

The premise is one question that the story answers. A good one has three properties.

- It can be answered. "Is the world doomed?" cannot. "Will the saint reach the shrine?" can.
- It has a price. The answer is never only yes or no; it is yes at a cost, or no and what follows.
- It makes you want to know. If you could write the ending today and feel nothing, change the question.

Write it as a sentence you could say aloud to a friend. It will end up on the first page of your flow.

## The tone

Tone is a promise about feelings, and you keep it with the tables you write. Choose three words and one thing the story is not. "Grim, tender, slow; not heroic" tells you what to put in your oracle tables and what to leave out.

Tone shows in the weights. A table that is all trouble wears you down by the third roll. A table where most results are quiet makes the bad ones land. Give the quiet results the most chances, and the trouble means something.

## The length

Say how many sessions the story takes, and say what ends it. Do not end it with "when I get bored". End it with a clock: a number that climbs until something has to happen. A story with a clock has a middle that moves and an end you cannot talk yourself out of. The next chapter shows how a clock closes a phase.

## On paper

Take a page of the notebook and call it the **promise card**. Write on it:

1. The premise, as one question.
2. Three words for the tone, and one thing it is not.
3. The length, in sessions.
4. What will end the story, as a clock you can draw: a row of boxes you fill in.

Do not write the answer to the premise. You will not know it yet, and the flow exists to find it. Keep the card open beside you at every session. When a scene drifts, read the premise and bring it back.

## The Long Carry

**The premise.** _Will you get the saint to the shrine before first snow, and what will it cost?_ It asks about a deadline and a price, so every scene is either a step toward the shrine or a payment on the way.

**The tone.** Weary, devout, unfair. The flow is not heroic: nobody carries the saint through a storm and wins. Its tables are full of small things going wrong, such as a stiff frost or a bad foot, and few things going well.

**The length.** Three to four sessions, kept by a clock called Winter. It starts empty and has ten segments. Every round of the road adds one, so the carry cannot go on for ever.

**Surprise.** The Road Risks table is rolled after every scene. It has nine chances: three for "A quiet road. Nothing happens that you can name", and one each for the six results that change something. The quiet result is the most common one on purpose.

**Memory.** Two Trackers (Winter and Burden) and three Facts (the saint, the Sin, the vow) are the whole book-keeping.

A played moment. A road scene has just ended. The player rolls Road Risks.

> The table gives: _A bearer's foot goes bad, and the others have to take the weight._ Burden goes up by one.
>
> I decide it is Ansel, who has been limping since the ford. Nobody says it. I write: "Ansel's foot. We shorten the day."

The table did not tell the player which bearer. It only said that something heavy had landed. The player chose the name, and the scene that came out of it belongs to both.

## Cold Berth

**The premise.** _What is aboard, who let it on, and why were we woken?_ This is a question about a mystery, and its price is paid in time: the longer you search, the closer the ship gets to showing you.

**The tone.** Cold, dry, watchful. The ship is not hostile; it follows rules written by someone else. The flow is not a monster story: the danger is that the ship is too orderly and no one explains.

**The length.** Two to three sessions, kept by a clock called Threat, with eight segments. It rises by one every round. From the fifth segment, the Reveal may come at the end of any scene.

**Surprise.** Three oracle tables hold the answers to the three questions, and a fourth, Ship trouble, is rolled every round. Like Road Risks, it keeps three of its nine chances for "A quiet hour. The ship ticks and settles."

**Memory.** Two Trackers (Threat and Clues) and three Facts (what is aboard, who let it on, why you were woken).

A played moment. The round has turned and the player rolls Ship trouble.

> The table gives: _A sound in the walls that stops when you listen._ The ship wants a Hide scene next.
>
> I had planned to look at the cargo. The table says I do not get to. I write: "Something moves in the wall of the Cold Hold. It stops when I stop. I need somewhere to be quiet."

The table took the next decision away from the player. That is what a good game master does: it makes the plan fail in a way that is interesting.

## Compare the two

Both promises ask a question. The Long Carry's question is about whether and at what price; Cold Berth's is about what and who. A "whether" story needs a deadline, so its clock is the calendar. A "what" story needs a reveal, so its clock is the ship's patience.

Neither says how it ends. The flow keeps the promise by making the ending depend on what you do, and on a number you can see.

## Try it

Write your own promise card now. Check it with three questions.

- Could a friend tell, from the premise alone, what a scene in this story is about?
- Does the tone rule out something you would otherwise do?
- Does the clock stop the story, or does it only decorate it?

If you answer yes three times, move to The Arc.
