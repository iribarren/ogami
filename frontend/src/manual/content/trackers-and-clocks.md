# Trackers and Clocks at the Table

Part I explained why a flow keeps numbers and what each kind is for. On paper you also have to draw them, keep them honest in the middle of a scene and know what to do when one runs out.

A **Tracker** is only useful if you look at it, so the best Tracker is the one you cannot help reading every time you turn the page.

## Drawing them

**A clock** is a row of boxes. Draw one box per segment and put a number under every second one, so that you can find your place at a glance. Mark the box where the dice start with a small flag or a different colour, because when you reach it the loop changes character, and you want to see that coming.

**A counter** is a line with the numbers on it and a ring around the current one, with the names of its stretches written under the line. Burden counts from 0 to 6, and its stretches are Light, Heavy, Crushing and Breaking. A name tells you more than a number does, so read the name first.

Keep every Tracker for one flow on the same page, at the top, where you will see it before anything else, and write the hint of each one, the line that says what it measures, beside it.

## What fills them

Before play, write down what moves each Tracker. A Tracker moves from one of three sources. The World turn moves the clock by one every time, whatever you did. A table can move one too, when a line says **(Winter +1)** and you mark it. Checks and steps are the third source, as when a failed check costs a box or a step says to add one.

If something moves a Tracker and you cannot say which of these it was, you have made up a rule. That is allowed, but write it down so that you do it the same way next time.

Mark a Tracker the moment the cause happens, and not at the end of the scene, because a box you leave to mark later is a box you forget.

## When one fills

Decide before you begin what a full clock will do, and write it on the page. A full clock can end a Phase, as **The Road** ends when the snow comes and **The Ship** ends when the Threat clock is full. It can force a scene instead, by sending you to the scene you were avoiding. Or it can pick an ending, through the Tracker's final value, and the **Epilogue** of each flow reads two Trackers and chooses from them.

A counter does not fill, it moves, so decide what its extremes mean. Burden at its top is a bearer who cannot walk. Clues at its top is a whole picture, and the **Epilogue** reads it.

When a Tracker has reached its top, leave it there, and do not carry the overflow into another Tracker unless a rule says so.

### Going down

Some Trackers can fall. In Cold Berth a Repair that is done takes a box off Threat, and that is the only way the clock falls. Make a falling Tracker rare and make it pay for a scene, because if it falls often the clock stops being a clock.

## Keeping them honest

A Tracker you stop trusting stops working, and these habits keep it honest.

- **Say the number out loud when it moves.** "Winter, five." It takes a second and catches the box you forgot.
- **Never move one to be kind to yourself.** If the clock says the road is over, the road is over, and a game that rescues you teaches you nothing.
- **Look back at the end of a session.** Count the marks against the journal, since a mismatch is a scene you did not write down.

## On paper

Draw the page once for the whole flow, and copy it to a fresh page for each session with the marks carried over. At the end of every session, write the date, the state of each Tracker and one line of what changed.

Keep two cards beside the page, on index cards or on a page of your notebook, to make the rest quick. The **World turn card** is a checklist you read after every scene, and the **closing card** says what each clock does when it fills.

:::in-ogami
In Studio, a Tracker is declared on the **Trackers & tags** page with **New tracker**: a **Key**, a **Name**, a **Hint** and a **Kind**, **Counter** or **Clock**. A clock has **Segments**. A counter has **Min**, **Max**, **Initial** and optional levels. A step's **Add effect** can add to a Tracker. A **condition** step reads a Tracker and sends the flow down a band, and a band can carry an `endPhase` effect.

In Play, the **Trackers** panel draws each Tracker as filled and empty boxes, with its level label and its hint. You can move one by hand with the **Decrease** and **Increase** buttons, or type a number and press **Set**. What happens at the last box is the flow's business, through a condition step, and not the panel's.
:::

## The Long Carry

Winter is a clock of ten, and Burden a counter from 0 to 6 with four stretches. The Long Carry marks Winter every turn, and Burden whenever the bearers take on weight, as with a bad foot, a favour or a ford that goes badly.

On paper the page is simple, with a row of ten boxes for Winter and a flag on the sixth, and a line from 0 to 6 for Burden with Light, Heavy, Crushing and Breaking under it.

At the ford at the end of a hard day the player makes a Back check and rolls a 20.

> Going badly. The ford takes d6 Wind, and Burden goes up by one, from 1 to 2. That is the top of Heavy. I draw the ring around the 2 and write the stretch beside it.

One more, and the bearers are Crushing. The name changes before the number gets frightening.

## Cold Berth

Threat is a clock of eight, and Clues a counter from 0 to 8 with the stretches Blind, Hints, A shape and The whole picture.

Threat fills for good, since once it is full the closing check ends **The Ship**. It can also fall, but only through a Repair. Clues only climbs, and it is read at the end, in the **Epilogue**, together with Threat, to choose how the crew gets out.

In a Repair on a failed scrubber, the crew member's Station gives a d8, rolled against Difficulty 3. The player rolls a 6, and the outcome band is Done.

> Threat comes down by one, from five to four. That brings it to the edge of the quiet stretch. I cross out the old mark in the fifth box and write what has changed: the scrubbers hold.

The fall cost a scene, and it bought a scene's worth of quiet.

## Try it

Draw the Trackers of your flow on one page. For each, write what moves it, what it does when it reaches the top, and the name of the stretch that makes you nervous.
